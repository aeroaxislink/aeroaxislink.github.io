The Domestic Before Continental Charter

A sequencing theory for African air cargo, and the test we use to apply it.

What we mean

Domestic before Continental is a sequencing theory. It says that a continental air route is only as strong as the domestic markets at each of its ends, and that the order in which things are built matters.

A route between two capitals carries what two countries put on it. If the farms, fisheries, clinics and exporters inland cannot reach their own national airport reliably, there is little for the continental flight to collect. If they can, the same flight fills in both directions. The domestic network is where cargo is gathered and where it is delivered. The continental route is the trunk. The trunk needs branches.

The theory is a hypothesis we are testing in public, one airstrip at a time. We state it plainly so that it can be checked, challenged and corrected.

What it is not

It is not an argument against continental growth. Today we study regional airstrips. Continental routes, such as Addis Ababa to Dubai or Lagos to Accra, come next, and we will read them through the same lens. It is not a call to delay open skies, or to put a country’s regional ambitions on hold. It is not a criticism of any authority, airline or county. Kenya Airports Authority, the Kenya Civil Aviation Authority, county governments and operators are doing patient work, often in difficult conditions, and we say so each time.

It is a question of order and of evidence. Build the branches, then judge the trunk by what the branches can feed it.

How this differs from SAATM

The Single African Air Transport Market is a continental policy framework. It sets out to liberalise air services between African states, so that qualifying carriers can fly more freely between countries. We support the goal. Its subject is the rules between countries.

Domestic before Continental asks a different question. It does not ask whether a route is permitted. It asks whether a route has something to carry. Policy opens a door; demand and supply decide who walks through it. Liberalisation can allow a flight, but only a functioning domestic chain, from farm gate or factory to airstrip and from airport to buyer, gives that flight a reason to be full.

So the two ideas are complements. SAATM is about access. Our theory is about readiness. We believe the market it describes works best where the domestic markets it connects are strong, and we would welcome evidence that shows otherwise.

Why it matters for cargo

Passenger routes can survive on a single end. A tourist city can fill a seat to a capital. Cargo is different. An aircraft earns money when it is full in both directions, and cargo needs conditions at both ends: a buyer, a cold room or a warehouse, a road to the airstrip, a right-sized aircraft, and something to fly back.

This is why a corridor can be announced and still disappoint. The announcement is about the route. The outcome depends on what happens in the first and last kilometres. For many African producers, the first mile is the weakest link, so the first question is domestic.

It is also why small steps matter. A service that starts small and proves itself, then grows, is usually a better foundation than a large one that has to be filled.

The method: the Lift Test

We apply the theory with the Lift Test. It is an editorial framework, not an official rating. Seven questions are scored from zero to two: value per kilogram, time, the road gap, an anchor buyer, a backhaul, passengers who share the cost, and aircraft fit.

Each fact is tagged: S for sourced, E for our estimate, ? for unknown. Where public evidence is missing we score conservatively and mark the question, and the score rises when the evidence appears. Authorities validate facts, not scores. The bands are People-first, Conditional and Cargo-led, and none of them is a failure. A strip that does a different job well is doing its job.

We have applied it to Kerenga, Lodwar, Malindi, Garissa and Nanyuki, each from public information, each as a draft, each open to correction.

The test for continental routes

The same discipline extends upward. For any continental route, we ask one question: does it have a domestic anchor?

At each end, is there a named buyer or shipper with steady volume? Is there a cold chain or a warehouse? Does a domestic leg carry cargo to the gateway airport? Can the aircraft fly something both ways? A route with a yes at both ends is a route we expect to last. A route with an open question at one end is a route we watch, and we say which question is open.

This is how we stay consistent without being narrow. When we turn to continental routes such as Addis Ababa to Dubai or Lagos to Accra, the lens will not change. Continental and domestic are one chain, read in order.

Where we could be wrong

A theory that cannot fail is not a theory. Some continental routes may succeed without a strong domestic base, for example where a single large shipper supplies the volume. Some strong domestic strips may never need a continental link. A hub carrier’s network may also make the order less strict than we describe. If we find such cases, we will publish them, and we will refine the sequence rather than defend it.

Our evidence so far is a small set of airstrips, scored from public information. That is a start, not a proof. We would rather say so than claim more.

Put the clock on it

A plan without a date is a hope. When an airline, authority or county says it will do something in public, we record what was said, by whom and by when, and we return on that date. We say plainly when a date is met, and we set out what would have to be true when it is not. We credit the people who gave us the idea: the phrase comes from RwandAir’s Chief Executive, Yvonne Makolo.

Clocks are not criticism. They are how a plan becomes visible, and how the people keeping to it get credit.

How we work

We are independent and we publish our sources. We use public information and say so. We pair every claim with a tag. We thank the institutions whose work we study. We publish corrections and we welcome them.

If you manage an airstrip, run a county, fly a route or ship goods, and you can tell us what we have wrong, write to us. A better fact makes the score fairer, and a fairer score makes the argument stronger.

Build the branches. Then judge the trunk.
